# Product Discovery

Use discovery to decide whether a product or capability deserves investment before optimizing its implementation.

## Frame the decision

- Define the product shape: B2B, B2C, B2B2C, marketplace, internal product, or another model.
- Separate the economic buyer, champion, administrator, operator, end user, and data subject.
- State the job, current alternative, triggering event, desired outcome, and cost of the problem.
- Record constraints: geography, channels, regulation, sensitive data, integrations, support model, availability, latency, and budget.
- Define explicit non-goals, limitations, risk appetite, and kill criteria.

## Calibrate before broad research

- On a greenfield, rebuild, or "from zero" request, first reflect the known vision and ask whether the user is resetting code, product assumptions, or both.
- Ask at most three material questions at a time. Do not fill missing product intent with competitor research, legacy assumptions, or model invention.
- Keep three layers explicit: product identity and core capability, go-to-market wedge, and first release or implementation slice. Never promote one layer into another without a human decision.
- Treat memory, deleted work, branches, and prior documentation as provisional evidence unless the user explicitly carries the decision forward.
- Do only enough local orientation to frame the decision. Do not spawn research subagents while a direct user answer is more authoritative, faster, and cheaper; begin broader research once its questions can change an identified decision.
- Preserve the repository unchanged until the product foundation is accepted or the user explicitly asks for a discovery artifact.

## Inspect before inventing

- Audit the current repository, product surfaces, analytics, support evidence, prior decisions, and abandoned work.
- Research current primary sources: direct interviews and observation, official statistics and regulators, public filings, first-party competitor products, documentation, packaging, pricing, and terms.
- Use secondary analysis to discover leads or triangulate, not as the sole basis for a material claim.
- Date evidence. Distinguish observed facts, inferred patterns, assumptions, and unanswered questions.

## Evaluate the market

- Segment by shared problem, buying motion, constraints, and willingness to change rather than broad demographics.
- Compare alternatives on target customer, core job, workflow, proof, onboarding, distribution, value metric, packaging, price, limitations, and switching cost.
- Include spreadsheets, agencies, internal processes, and doing nothing as competitors.
- Estimate market size bottom-up only when it changes a decision. Show customer count, reachable share, price or value metric, sales capacity, and uncertainty.
- Identify why the opportunity exists now and what could make it unattractive.

## Build evidence-backed personas

- Create the minimum number of behavioral profiles needed for decisions.
- Record goals, workflows, context, constraints, triggers, objections, authority, success signals, and current workarounds.
- Avoid fictional biographies and decorative demographics.
- Keep buyer, user, and affected third party separate when their incentives differ.

## Shape value and pricing

- Express the value proposition as: target customer, important job, painful alternative, differentiated outcome, and credible proof.
- Choose a value metric that grows with customer value and remains understandable, measurable, and hard to game.
- Test packaging against distinct needs and operational costs; avoid tiers made only from arbitrary feature withholding.
- Evaluate willingness to pay, competitor anchors, gross margin, support cost, expansion path, billing complexity, and abuse incentives.
- Treat every price as a hypothesis until tested with behavior or direct buyer evidence.

## Decide

- Produce a concise product foundation: target segment, actors, problem, outcome, alternative, value proposition, business model, value metric, constraints, limitations, evidence, assumptions, and success or kill thresholds.
- Recommend `proceed`, `research`, `hold`, or `stop` with reasons.
- Ask the user to decide only where product judgment or risk appetite is material.
- Revisit discovery when evidence changes; do not defend sunk cost.

Use [GOV.UK discovery guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/user-research-in-discovery) as an authoritative example of researching users and end-to-end needs before planning or building. Adapt the method to the product; do not copy government ceremony.
